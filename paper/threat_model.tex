\section{Threat Model: Roles, Data Planes, and Outcomes}\label{sec:threat}

We model failures of delegated payment authority, not ``fraudulent agents'' as
an actor class.  An adversary may be a malicious principal, a party that
compromises an otherwise legitimate agent, a malicious tool or counterparty, or
a set of colluding controllers.  The agent may therefore be an instrument, a
victim, or a participant.  A financial-crime conclusion additionally requires
evidence of the relevant outcome.

\subsection{Scope and Preconditions}

The observed environment is a public blockchain settlement plane, optionally
joined to control- and wallet-plane telemetry.  We assume that an agent can
invoke tools only when a deployment grants that capability, and that a transfer
can settle only when a wallet or custodian authorizes a valid transaction.
Session orchestration APIs such as those documented by OpenClaw
\cite{openclaw2026} demonstrate control-plane composition; they are not payment
primitives and do not by themselves create wallets, keys, balances, or signed
transactions.  Account-abstraction and agent-payment designs make the missing
authorization layer explicit\cite{erc4337,ap2protocol2025,zhang2026sok}.

The protected assets are (i) mandate integrity, (ii) instruction and tool-call
provenance, (iii) wallet credentials and policy, (iv) funds and market
integrity, and (v) evidence needed for investigation.  Consensus attacks,
cryptographic breaks, and physical coercion are outside scope.  We also do not
assume that registry membership, automation, high velocity, or cross-chain
activity is harmful.

\subsection{CHAIN\_3: High-Rate Execution}\label{subsec:chain3}

Automation can compress the interval between instruction and attempted action.
That is an amplifier, not a complete attack.  The causal scenario is:

\begin{center}
compromised or malicious instruction $\rightarrow$ authorized payment tool
$\rightarrow$ repeated wallet approvals $\rightarrow$ settlement and harm.
\end{center}

The necessary preconditions are an accessible payment tool, signing authority,
funds, and a rail that accepts the transactions.  Wallet limits, nonce rules,
gas, service quotas, and policy checks can all bind throughput.  A
fire-and-forget messaging call establishes none of these facts.  Conversely,
high throughput can be legitimate in market making, payroll, treasury, or
machine-to-machine settlement.  Detection therefore requires mandate deviation
or outcome evidence in addition to rate.

Useful controls exist primarily before settlement: per-mandate value and count
limits, beneficiary scopes, contract allowlists, transaction simulation,
step-up authorization, and rapid revocation.  Settlement velocity may support
triage, but a human-rate threshold is neither necessary nor sufficient for
fraud.

\subsection{CHAIN\_4: Disposable Identity or Wallet Fan-Out}\label{subsec:chain4}

Creating an agent process is not the same as creating a financially capable
identity.  A Sybil payment scenario requires distinct accounts or credentials,
funding, and authority to transact.  Regulated accounts can also impose
onboarding and beneficial-owner controls even when software instances are cheap.
The classical Sybil problem therefore remains relevant\cite{douceur2002}, but
its cost must be measured at the wallet, account, and funding layers rather
than inferred from session creation.

The settlement-plane hypothesis is a group of recently activated addresses
linked by common funding, beneficiaries, contract interactions, or timing.
Those features can support entity resolution or coordinated-abuse detection;
they do not establish intent on their own.  Legitimate distributors, relayers,
batch processors, and account-abstraction infrastructure are necessary benign
comparators.  Cleanup of an off-chain session does not erase immutable
settlement records, although it may remove useful control-plane provenance if
logs are not retained under an appropriate policy.

\subsection{CHAIN\_6: Behavioral Mimicry}\label{subsec:chain6}

Mimicry is an evasion strategy applied to another threat, not a standalone
financial crime.  An adaptive executor can reduce conspicuous regularity by
sampling times, amounts, and counterparties from conditional distributions.
Deterministic low-variance replay is one possible strategy, but modern software
can also use pseudorandom or learned stochastic policies.  It is therefore not
generally true that machine imitation must be ``too perfect.''

The current injection tests only a deliberately regular generator.  Its
detection shows that the implementation responds to that signature; it does
not establish an impossibility result for mimicry.  A credible evaluation must
hold out attack generators, include adaptive stochastic policies, and test
whether the signal adds value at a fixed false-positive budget.  Provenance,
mandate, and wallet-policy evidence is less dependent on behavioral appearance
than timing alone.

\subsection{CHAIN\_7: Coordinated Multi-Entity Execution}\label{subsec:chain7}

Coordination can distribute activity so that each address has little history.
This creates a unit-of-analysis problem for the present per-address scorer: a
collective pattern cannot be inferred from isolated address histories.  The
appropriate unit may instead be an entity cluster, funding component,
beneficiary neighborhood, or synchronized event window.  Graph-based fraud and
AML research already provides relevant methods\cite{pourhabibi2020,wei2021};
the empirical question is whether they improve detection for a specified
agentic-payment outcome.

Simultaneity and equal amounts are not sufficient evidence of collusion.
Airdrops, payroll, bridges, market infrastructure, and batched contract calls
can generate similar patterns.  A collective detector therefore needs benign
process labels, controller or funding linkage, and an independently established
harm predicate.

\subsection{Remaining Scenario Handles}\label{subsec:chains_other}

\paragraph{CHAIN\_1: Enumeration.}
Registry or session discovery can be intended platform behavior.  Unauthorized
enumeration is a control-plane access problem.  Unless the discovery operation
itself emits a transaction, transfer topology cannot observe it; a dust-transfer
proxy tests a different behavior.

\paragraph{CHAIN\_2: Context or history extraction.}
The relevant evidence is an authorization decision and access log for private
context.  Repeated transfers to one address do not demonstrate that a history
API was read.  Any later fraud or market-abuse claim must link the access to a
separate harmful action.

\paragraph{CHAIN\_5: Cross-domain identity and value movement.}
The same controller may legitimately operate across chains, venues, or payment
rails.  Cross-domain activity becomes relevant only under a specified hypothesis
such as laundering, sanctions evasion, account compromise, or inconsistent
mandate.  Testing that hypothesis requires entity linkage and outcome context;
a single-chain micro-payment pattern is not a substitute.

\paragraph{CHAIN\_8: Manipulation or laundering.}
Market manipulation and money laundering must not be merged into a generic
``fraud'' class.  Wash-trading analysis requires trades, venue mechanics,
beneficial-control hypotheses, and market effects\cite{victor2021}.  Layering
analysis instead concerns source, destination, intermediaries, and criminal
proceeds.  Transfer-only features can generate leads, but adjudication depends
on the typology-specific evidence plane.

\subsection{Evidence Boundaries and Falsification}

Each scenario is falsifiable at its claimed level.  A documented API supports
capability only if its stated preconditions are met.  A synthetic pattern
supports detector response only for that generator.  Registry-associated
separation supports attribution only for the sampled owners and counterparties.
Fraud, AML, or market-abuse effectiveness requires independent outcome labels.
If joined mandate and wallet evidence shows that high-rate or coordinated
transactions were authorized and legitimate, actor type and behavioral
regularity must not override that evidence.  This ordering prevents an
attribution score from becoming a proxy accusation.
