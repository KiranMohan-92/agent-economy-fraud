\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

The empirical contribution of this study is narrower than agent-fraud
detection.  The real labels distinguish addresses associated with ERC-8004
registrations from selected non-registered counterparties.  They do not verify
that the counterparties are human, establish autonomous control for every
observed transaction, or identify fraudulent behavior.  The measured task is
therefore registered-identity owner discrimination.  The current features show
modest settlement-layer signal for that task, while the injected scenarios show
only that the implementation responds to the signatures it was designed to
score.

The durable security result is causal.  Agentic payments join controller intent,
mandate, instruction provenance, tool invocation, wallet authorization,
settlement, and outcome.  Loss becomes possible when that chain is broken or
cannot be reconstructed.  Automation can amplify the speed, scale, delegation
depth, and coordination of a failure, but automation and registry status are
not evidence of fraud.  The same broken chain can matter when the executor is an
LLM agent, conventional software, a compromised application, or a person.

This causal model also determines the relevant evidence.  Control-plane abuse
requires instruction and tool logs; unauthorized payment requires wallet-policy
and mandate evidence; settlement analysis requires transaction and entity
graphs; fraud, AML, and market-abuse conclusions require distinct outcome and
adjudication data.  No single on-chain score spans all four planes.

The next research step is consequently not deployment.  It is a sequence of
construct-valid studies: verify registry timing and owner labels, use
entity-grouped temporal evaluation, obtain independent fraud or financial-crime
outcomes, compare against appropriate baselines, and test held-out adaptive
attacks with legitimate automated twins.  Only a prospective shadow study that
demonstrates calibrated incremental value under operational and governance
constraints could support a production claim.

Reframed this way, the paper offers a useful early benchmark and a falsifiable
threat model.  It shows how to study agentic-payment risk without treating actor
type as culpability and without claiming more than the available evidence can
establish.
