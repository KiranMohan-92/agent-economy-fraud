\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

The present study sits at the intersection of agentic-payment security,
software-actor attribution, financial-crime detection, and blockchain
analytics.  These literatures answer different questions.  Protocol-security
work asks whether delegated actions are authorized; bot detection asks whether
an actor is automated; fraud and AML research asks whether activity satisfies a
harm or proceeds-related target; blockchain analytics asks what can be inferred
from public settlement data.  Our contribution is to keep those constructs
separate while auditing an early registered-identity dataset.

\subsection{Agentic Commerce and Payment Authorization}\label{subsec:rw-agentcommerce}

Recent systematizations organize agentic payments across discovery,
authorization, execution, and accounting layers.  Zhang~\etal
\cite{zhang2026sok} identify failures such as weak intent binding and misuse
under valid authorization; Mao~\etal\cite{mao2026sok} analyze threats spanning
ERC-8004, AP2, x402, and related systems.  This lifecycle view supports our
separation of control, wallet, settlement, and outcome planes.

Prevention-oriented designs address particular links in that chain.  AP2 uses
mandates to carry payment intent\cite{ap2protocol2025}; A402 binds payment
finalization to verified service execution\cite{li2026a402}; programmable
wallets can enforce transaction policies, limits, and step-up controls
\cite{erc4337,metamask2026agentwallet}.  These mechanisms do not eliminate
detection needs, but they show why actor type is not the primary causal
variable: a well-bound automated payment can be legitimate, whereas a broken
mandate or wallet policy can create loss under several executor types.

Our work is complementary.  It does not evaluate a payment protocol or prove
that a session platform settles payments.  OpenClaw is used only as an example
of control-plane orchestration\cite{openclaw2026}.  The threat model asks what
additional wallet and outcome evidence would be required before a documented
orchestration capability supported a financial-crime claim.

\subsection{AI Agent and Tool-Use Security}\label{subsec:rw-aisec}

Indirect prompt injection can cause an LLM-integrated application to execute
attacker-influenced instructions\cite{greshake2023}.  More broadly, agentic-AI
security research covers tool misuse, compromised dependencies, coordination,
and accountability\cite{lazer2026agentic,unit42openclaw2026}.  A documented
agent-wallet incident on Base illustrates the agent-as-victim pathway
\cite{grokbankr2026}.  Such evidence establishes that control-plane compromise
can reach a payment-capable deployment; it does not imply that registered
agents are generally malicious or validate every settlement-layer typology.

This literature motivates our role model: principal, agent executor, tool,
wallet, counterparty, and victim are distinct.  Security evidence must identify
which role was compromised and how authority propagated to settlement.

\subsection{Automation and Owner Attribution}\label{subsec:rw-botdetect}

Distinguishing software-mediated activity from human interaction has a long
history.  CAPTCHA research documents the erosion of challenge--response
assumptions\cite{bursztein2014}; Web bot detection combines timing, interaction,
and device features\cite{iliou2021,jonker2019}.  This work demonstrates that
automation can shift behavioral distributions, but also that the relevant
features are contingent on channel and adversary rather than universal
biological invariants.

Our real-data task is narrower still.  It distinguishes addresses associated
with one registry from a selected counterparty class.  Because the negative
class may contain other automation and the positive label does not prove
autonomous execution for every transaction, the result is best understood as
registered-identity owner discrimination.  It should not be compared directly
with a verified human-versus-bot benchmark.

\subsection{Fraud, AML, and Graph Detection}\label{subsec:rw-fraud}

Financial-fraud research combines supervised labels, anomaly detection, and
graph structure\cite{chandola2009,carcillo2021,hilal2022}.  Graph-based methods
are especially relevant for shared funding, common beneficiaries, and
population-level behavior\cite{pourhabibi2020,wei2021}.  These methods do not
depend exclusively on human embodiment; many already model relationships and
value flow across accounts or entities.

The comparison clarifies the boundary of our study.  Fraud requires labels for
unauthorized or deceptive harm.  AML concerns suspicious proceeds and
transaction structure under a different legal and investigative process.
Market abuse requires trading and market-impact evidence.  Registry association
is none of these targets.  The exploratory features in this paper may later be
tested as contextual covariates, but the present labels cannot measure their
incremental financial-crime value.

\subsection{Blockchain Analytics}\label{subsec:rw-blockchain}

Public-ledger research uses address clustering, transaction graphs, and
contract-call structure to infer entities and behavior
\cite{meiklejohn2016,chen2020}.  Specialized work detects wash-trading patterns
using trade and ownership hypotheses\cite{victor2021}.  These methods establish
what can be learned from the settlement plane while also exposing its limits:
an on-chain edge does not reveal the originating prompt, the applicable mandate,
service delivery, or investigative disposition.

Our empirical contribution is an early measurement of registry-associated
address separation in Base-chain USDC data.  The construct audit is as important
as the score: it identifies which upstream and downstream variables are absent
and therefore which causal conclusions cannot be drawn.

\subsection{Adversarial Evaluation}\label{subsec:rw-advml}

Adversarial-ML research shows that adaptive evaluation must distinguish model
performance from performance against a particular attack generator
\cite{biggio2013,biggio2018,cartella2021}.  An attack designed by the detector's
authors and encoded as a conspicuous synthetic signature is useful for checking
implementation response, but it is not an independent robustness test.  This
principle is especially important for behavioral mimicry: stochastic policies
can vary timing and amounts, so detection of deterministic low-variance replay
does not establish a general mimicry paradox.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
We make no claim to replace protocol controls, existing fraud systems, or graph
analytics.  The paper contributes (i) a construct audit of an early ERC-8004
transaction dataset, (ii) an exploratory registered-owner discrimination
benchmark, and (iii) a role- and data-plane threat model that states what
additional evidence would be needed for fraud, AML, market-abuse, or operational
claims.  This positioning is deliberately narrower than prior versions of the
manuscript and matches the evidence currently available.
