\section{Background and Construct Model}\label{sec:background}

This section defines the objects the data can identify, the planes on which an
agentic payment can fail, and the evidential standard used throughout the
paper.  The definitions prevent three substitutions that otherwise recur in
early agent-economy research: registered identity for autonomous control,
automation for fraud, and observable transfer pattern for financial-crime
outcome.

\subsection{Constructs, Not Biological Invariants}\label{subsec:invariants}

An \emph{agent} is software that selects or executes actions on behalf of a
principal.  A \emph{registered identity} is an entry in an identity system such
as ERC-8004\cite{erc8004}.  A \emph{wallet} controls signing authority over an
account.  These objects may be linked, but they are not interchangeable.  A
registry entry does not prove how an address was controlled at a particular
time; a wallet does not reveal the instruction that caused a signature; and a
transfer does not establish whether the underlying purpose was legitimate.

The positive label in our real-data study is operationally defined as an
address associated with an ERC-8004 registration.  The comparison class
contains selected counterparties that are not present in that registry.  We
therefore use \emph{registered-identity owner discrimination} as shorthand for
the measured task.  We do not call the comparison class human: it can contain
contracts, exchanges, relayers, conventional bots, or unregistered agents.
Nor do we call the positive class fraudulent: registration carries no such
meaning.

Four downstream constructs must also remain separate:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Attribution} asks what kind of owner, controller, or execution
    process is associated with an address.
  \item \textbf{Control-plane security} asks whether instructions, tools, or
    credentials were manipulated or used outside a mandate.
  \item \textbf{Fraud} requires an outcome construct such as unauthorized use,
    deception, or victim loss.
  \item \textbf{AML and market abuse} concern different outcomes---for example,
    handling criminal proceeds or manipulating a market---and require their own
    labels, data, and legal analysis.
\end{itemize}
An attribution signal can be a contextual feature in any of the latter tasks,
but it is not a substitute for their target labels.

Human-calibrated features such as circadian activity, interaction timing,
device continuity, and transaction velocity can shift when execution is
automated.  Bot-detection and anomaly-detection research has long documented
this distributional issue\cite{chandola2009,iliou2021,jonker2019}.  We treat
these features as contingent empirical regularities, not as a complete set of
biological invariants.  Software also has physical infrastructure, latency,
energy, budget, credential, and policy constraints; the relevant question is
which constraint is measured in a specified system.

\subsection{Control, Wallet, Settlement, and Outcome Planes}\label{subsec:platforms}

Agentic payment security spans four data planes.  Collapsing them creates false
causal claims because an event visible on one plane need not be visible on
another.

  \centering
  \caption{Data planes in an agentic payment and the evidence available on each.}
  \label{tab:data_planes}
  \small
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{2.4cm} p{4.1cm} X@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Plane} & \textbf{Representative evidence} & \textbf{Question answered} \\
    \midrule
    Control & mandate, prompt, message, tool call, model and policy version &
      What instruction was issued, by whom, and with what provenance? \\
    Wallet authorization & key custody, policy decision, simulation, signature,

    Settlement & transfer, trade, contract call, fee, block and counterparty graph &

  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

OpenClaw exposes session and orchestration primitives\cite{openclaw2026}.  Those
primitives are control-plane capabilities: by themselves they do not provision
a wallet, confer signing authority, or settle a blockchain transaction.  A
payment-capable deployment requires a separate tool and authorization path.
Likewise, account abstraction specifies programmable account execution
semantics\cite{erc4337}, while AP2 describes mandate-bearing payment flows
\cite{ap2protocol2025}.  These components may be composed, but evidence about
one does not establish behavior in another.

The plane distinction also explains the limits of public-chain data.  Transfer
graphs can support address attribution, flow analysis, and some entity-level
hypotheses\cite{meiklejohn2016,chen2020}.  They generally cannot reveal a hidden
prompt, whether a principal approved the action, whether the purchased service
was delivered, or whether an investigator later confirmed fraud.  Those facts
require joined control-, wallet-, or outcome-plane evidence.

\subsection{Roles and Accountability}

The executor's substrate is orthogonal to its role in an incident.  A useful
threat model distinguishes at least:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item the \emph{principal or controller} that defines the mandate and is the
    candidate accountable person or legal entity;
  \item the \emph{agent executor} that interprets or carries out instructions;
  \item the \emph{tool or service provider} that exposes external capability;
  \item the \emph{wallet or custodian} that enforces signing policy;
  \item a \emph{counterparty}, which may itself be a person, agent, contract, or
    institution;
  \item a \emph{victim or affected market}; and
  \item an \emph{investigator, venue, or regulated institution} that observes
    only part of the evidence chain.
\end{itemize}

An agent can be a legitimate delegate, an attacker-controlled instrument, a
compromised victim, or one participant in a colluding group.  A single incident
can include several of these roles.  Documented indirect prompt injection, for
example, demonstrates agent-as-victim risk\cite{greshake2023,grokbankr2026}; it
does not by itself validate a claim about autonomous agents acting as fraudulent
principals.

\subsection{Evidence Vector}

Evidence does not form a monotone ladder.  We therefore record eight
independent, time-stamped dimensions for each address, event, or case:
registry record ($R$), current verified wallet association ($W$), live service
($S$), authenticated autonomous-execution trace ($A$), commerce/payment chain
($C$), independently verified person/human control ($P$), independently
adjudicated task outcome ($Y$), and measured harm or intervention outcome
($H$). Each is present, absent, or unknown and carries a
source.  Claims must name the conjunction they require; no component implies
another.  Requirements are claim-specific: registry association needs $R$;
observed autonomous execution needs $A$ (authenticated execution provenance
with time-correct address or wallet linkage), and adds $W$ or $S$ only when the
scoped mechanism depends on them; a delegated-payment observation additionally
needs $C$; task performance needs $Y$; a verified-human comparator needs $P$;
and effectiveness or harm reduction needs $Y+H$.

The present real-data experiment supplies only $R$-loader evidence for its
positive cohort: selection through the loader's historical owner or
mint-recipient relation.  That does not by itself establish transaction-time
$R$, and it leaves $W,S,A,C,P,Y,H$ unknown. Its injected
scenarios test code response to generated motifs; they are not real-world
identity, execution, incident, or robustness evidence.  Public incidents can
support selected $Y$ or $H$ observations for adjacent control-plane failures,
but they do not fill missing vector components for the measured addresses.

\subsection{Hard-to-Vary Causal Thesis}\label{subsec:hardtovary}

The explanatory core is the preservation of delegated authority:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
Loss risk arises when controller intent and mandate cannot be traced through
instruction provenance and tool--wallet authorization to settlement and
outcome.  Automation changes the rate and scale at which such a failure can
propagate; it does not turn a legitimate actor into a fraudulent one.
\end{quote}

The account survives two decisive counterfactuals.  First, an automated payment
that remains within a verifiable mandate is not suspicious merely because it is
fast, precise, or continuous.  Second, the same broken authorization chain can
produce loss when the executor is a script or human-operated application rather
than an AI agent.  Removing mandate, provenance, authorization, settlement, or
outcome from the explanation removes a necessary causal or evidential link.
This functional dependence, rather than a fixed list of human traits, is what
makes the explanation hard to vary\cite{deutsch2011}.
