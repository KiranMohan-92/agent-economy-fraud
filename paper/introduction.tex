\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Agentic payment systems combine components that were historically easier to
separate: a principal states an objective, software interprets that objective,
tools expose external capabilities, a wallet authorizes value transfer, and a
payment rail settles the result.  Standards and implementations for agent
identity, delegation, and payment are emerging across these layers
\cite{erc8004,ap2protocol2025,zhang2026sok,mao2026sok}.  The resulting security
question is not whether software can transact faster than a person.  It is
whether a settled action remains bound to an authenticated principal, a
specific mandate, an authorized tool invocation, and an intended outcome.

This distinction is necessary for interpreting early on-chain evidence.  We
study a retained Base-chain USDC transfer snapshot whose label artifact contains
665 addresses associated with ERC-8004 registrations and 1,069 active
counterparties.  (The historical transaction extraction and an earlier
548-address positive set came from a different registry snapshot; we do not
pair their counts with this label artifact.)  The available labels distinguish
registry-associated addresses from selected non-registered counterparties;
they do not establish that the counterparties are human, that every registered
address was autonomously controlled throughout the observation window, or that
any address committed fraud.  The empirical task is therefore
\emph{registered-identity owner discrimination}, not human-versus-agent
classification and not fraud detection.  Registry status is an identity-plane
observation.  Fraud requires outcome evidence such as unauthorized action,
deception, victim loss, or adjudication.

The paper consequently separates four data planes.  The \emph{control plane}
records instructions, mandates, messages, and tool calls.  The \emph{wallet
plane} records key custody, policy evaluation, signing, and revocation.  The
\emph{settlement plane} records transfers and trades.  The \emph{outcome plane}
records delivery, authorization disputes, loss, investigations, and confirmed
financial-crime outcomes.  Settlement data alone may reveal behavioral or
graph structure, but it cannot recover every upstream instruction or downstream
outcome.

Our core explanatory claim is correspondingly causal rather than ontological:

\begin{quote}
\itshape
Financial loss becomes possible when the chain from controller intent and
mandate, through instruction provenance and tool--wallet authorization, to
settlement and outcome is not preserved.  Automation can amplify speed, scale,
delegation depth, and coordination, but actor type is not itself evidence of
fraud.
\end{quote}

This explanation is hard to vary in Deutsch's sense\cite{deutsch2011} because
each link has a distinct function.  If mandate, provenance, authorization, and
outcome remain aligned, machine-speed activity can be legitimate.  If a link
fails, unauthorized loss can occur whether the executor is an LLM agent, a
conventional script, a compromised application, or a person.  The explanation
therefore predicts both the relevant control points and the evidence required
to test them.

The paper makes five bounded contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Construct audit.} We identify what the released labels do and
    do not support, and reinterpret the empirical task as discrimination of
    registry-associated owners from selected counterparties.
  \item \textbf{Role- and data-plane threat model.} We distinguish principals,
    agents, tools, wallets, counterparties, victims, and accountable entities,
    and map threat hypotheses to the control, wallet, settlement, and outcome
    planes (\cref{sec:threat}).
  \item \textbf{Exploratory attribution benchmark.} We evaluate whether
    settlement-layer features contain signal about registry association while
    treating the result as attribution evidence rather than fraud evidence
    (\cref{sec:detection,sec:evaluation}).
  \item \textbf{Evidence vector.} We keep registry, wallet, service,
    execution, payment, adjudication, and harm evidence independent and require
    each claim to state its necessary conjunction.
  \item \textbf{Validation and control agenda.} We specify the evidence and
    governance gates required before attribution features could inform a
    production control, without recommending deployment from the present data
    (\cref{sec:recommendations}).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper defines the constructs and causal model
(\cref{sec:background}), presents the threat hypotheses (\cref{sec:threat}),
describes the exploratory signals (\cref{sec:detection}), and reports their
evaluation (\cref{sec:evaluation}).  \Cref{sec:related} positions the work
relative to agentic-commerce security, fraud detection, and blockchain
analytics.  \Cref{sec:recommendations} gives validation and control
recommendations, \cref{sec:limitations} states the remaining validity limits,
and \cref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the supported conclusion.
